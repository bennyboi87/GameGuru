The short version

The website is a Shopify toy shop with café pages attached. The business is a two-site restaurant, bar, play venue and events operator that also has a shop.

The "Book" button works in Chrome, but it relies on a script, and the "Book Now" headings on six pages have nothing visible under them. There is no page for parties, none for corporate events, and no way to send a party or corporate enquiry. The kitchen and bar, which are probably the biggest earners per visit, sit behind PDF menus on a page called "cafe". Organised play is the strongest part of the site, but it has gaps: Pokémon is still "coming soon", the events have no prices, and two links go to the wrong place.

The fix is not a reskin. The site needs to be rebuilt around what people come to do: eat and drink, play, compete, celebrate, and take something home. Each of those needs a clear page and a way to book or buy on the spot.

What works

There is a lot to keep. These are the foundations the overhaul builds on.

A sound platform

Shopify gives you secure checkout, customer accounts, reliable hosting and a big app ecosystem. Nothing here needs a platform migration.

A deep range

The board games collection alone lists 372 items, with sale pricing, stock status, price filters and nine sort options. The product photography is high quality.

Credible organised play

The weekly schedule covers One Piece, Lorcana and Star Wars: Unlimited at both venues, with registration through Bandai TCG+, Ravensburger Play Hub and Melee. TCG players trust these platforms.

Concrete membership tiers

Casual Player (£30 a month), Core Gamer (£45 a month) and The Golden Ticket (£350 a year, limited to 300) are easy to understand, and the scarcity on the Golden Ticket is a good hook.

Clear basics

Both addresses, both phone numbers and simple hours (10am to 10pm every day) appear on every page. The hero line "Play. Collect. Café." is short and memorable.

A good story to tell

450+ games, a full kitchen, cocktails, an app with table ordering, and a second, larger venue that opened in 2026. The press coverage is better at saying this than the website is.

What doesn't work

Ordered by how much revenue or trust each problem costs. The evidence is taken from the live pages on the audit date.

High

Booking works, but only for tables, and only once scripts load

  • The "Book" button works in Chrome (confirmed by the owners' team). In the page code it points to #book, which doesn't exist, so the booking only appears once a script runs. Test it in Safari on iPhone, in Firefox, and inside the Instagram and TikTok in-app browsers, where scripts often behave differently.
  • Six pages have a "Book Now" heading with nothing visible underneath it until a script fills it in. Search engines and some screen readers only see the empty heading.
  • No booking path for parties, groups, corporate events or event tickets. Those still mean a phone call, and the phone numbers aren't tappable on mobile.
Critical

Parties and corporate events are invisible

  • No page, package, price, photo or form for kids' birthdays, adult celebrations, team building, private hire or buyouts.
  • The contact form's "query type" only offers General, Utilita Arena and Touchwood. There is no way to tell you about a 40-person corporate booking.
  • These are the highest-value bookings a venue like this takes, and today they depend on word of mouth and phone calls.
Critical

The restaurant and bar are undersold

  • The page is called "cafe", and the site-wide description talks about "comfort food". The press describes wood-fired pizza, craft burgers, bao, katsu, brunch, speciality coffee and cocktails.
  • The food, drinks and kids' menus are PDFs only. That means no prices on the page, poor reading on phones, nothing for Google to index, and no allergen or dietary labels on the page.
  • Nothing says what is different between the two venues, or invites people in to eat even if they don't want to play a game.
  • The Utilita Arena venue sits next to a major concert venue, and the site makes no pre-show or event-day pitch at all.
High

The homepage sells diecast cars ahead of the venue

  • Below the hero, the homepage is mostly shop: 12 category tiles, then the same eight products twice, once under "Game shop" and again under "Merch shop".
  • Those eight products look alphabetical rather than chosen (1969 Ford Mustang, 1994 Honda Del Sol, then Topps boxes). It reads as a default setting, not a curated shelf.
  • The only venue content is a ResDiary venue picker under "Reserve to Play", which only appears once a script runs. There is nothing on the homepage about events, parties, food, or what a visit costs.
  • The "Up to 15% off · Express Delivery" shop banner runs on every page, including café, venues and membership.
High

Play prices exist, but they're hard to find and don't sell membership

  • The Play page shows a clear price table: £4 for one hour, £6 for two and £8 for three, per person, with under-6s free and 6 to 14s at half price. But the whole table is a single image with no description, so Google can't read the prices and screen-reader users don't hear them.
  • The prices aren't linked to membership. At £6 for two hours, Casual Player (£30 a month) pays for itself after five two-hour visits a month, but nobody does that sum for the customer.
  • Nothing says whether food spend, children, peak times or event nights change the price.
  • The membership page says to download the app to subscribe, and also to "sign up to our newsletter to be the first to know when you can join". "Play every day (Mon–Fri)" contradicts itself. Nothing covers terms, cancellation, which venues are included, or how many Golden Tickets are left.
High

Events have holes that TCG players will notice

  • Pokémon, the biggest TCG by player count, shows "Coming Soon". Magic says "will be announced soon". Yu-Gi-Oh has no day or time.
  • Both Star Wars: Unlimited buttons point to the same Melee location page, so one venue is probably linked wrong. Both Yu-Gi-Oh buttons go to Konami product pages, not event registration.
  • No entry fees, formats, prize support, capacity, or calendar view. The organised play email address is plain text, not a link.
  • Social nights mentioned in the press (Play & Connect, Member Meetup, Social Game Night, Family & Friends Day) don't appear on the events page.
High

The shop is harder to browse than it should be

  • 31 collections, many of which overlap: "Family & Kids Games", "Family Favourites" and "Kids' Games"; "Miniature Games", "Miniatures" and "Hobby & Wargames"; three different strategy collections; two Hot Wheels URLs.
  • Filters are availability and price only. Board game buyers shop by player count, play time, age and complexity.
  • No click & collect, though you have two shops open 10am to 10pm every day. Collection gets people through the door, where they buy food and drink.
  • On the product page, there are no reviews, no delivery or returns information near the button, and an empty "You may also like" block. Nothing says "try it at our tables first". The buttons show leaked state text ("Add Added"), and the Azul title shows a stray "€" encoding error.
  • TCG product is sealed only, with no singles and no pre-order terms. See TCG singles.
High

Google can't see most of what you do

  • The browser-tab title is just "GameGuru". Every page uses the same café meta description, including shop and product pages. It says "Birmingham" only, and it's missing a comma ("…Café Birmingham enjoy board games…").
  • No separate pages for each venue, so no page can rank for "board game café Solihull" or "restaurant near Utilita Arena".
  • The News blog has no posts. Menus are in PDFs. The library list loads by script, so search engines may not see any of it.
  • The social share image is listed as 27×35 pixels and served over http, so shared links look broken on Facebook and WhatsApp.
High

The site is slow on phones, where most visitors are

  • Lighthouse mobile scores are low on every page tested: home 30, play 40, café 40, events 30, shop 33, venues 38, membership 31, library 43 (out of 100, where 90 or more is good). On desktop the same pages score between 38 and 82.
  • On a phone, the main image or heading takes between 5 and 24 seconds to appear. Google counts 2.5 seconds as good and anything over 4 seconds as poor. The page also freezes while scripts run, for between 1.2 and 7.3 seconds, so taps on Book or the menu do nothing at first.
  • The biggest causes are a 3 MB banner image on the homepage, collection images several times larger than the phone needs on the shop page (about 2.7 MB could be saved), two or three copies of the ResDiary booking widget loaded on every page whether or not anyone books, and a heavy page-builder and header script. Pages weigh 3.5 to 7 MB on a phone, and the library weighs 12 MB on desktop.
  • The shop page jumps about as it loads (layout shift of 0.22 on desktop and 0.11 on mobile, where 0.1 is the limit for good).
  • Lighthouse's automated checks also flag low colour contrast, links with no name, headings out of order, and tap targets that are too small. Its accessibility scores of 82 to 91 only cover what a machine can check, and the manual findings above still stand.
  • On a phone, the "Ask the Guru" chat button sits over the page content, covering text and the homepage Book button, and the shop banner wraps onto three lines above the logo.
Medium

Venue pages lack the information visitors need

  • One photo each, plus address and hours. No parking, public transport, step-free access, toilets, baby-changing, capacity, play areas, bar or kitchen details.
  • The Touchwood directions link is labelled "Alioli", which is a different business. The Utilita Arena map link points at the postcode, not the venue.
  • On the Play page, both venues use the same photo.
  • No holiday or event-day opening hours.
Medium

Accessibility falls short

  • Image descriptions (alt text) are empty on almost every page. Where they exist, some are filenames ("IMG_8080.jpg").
  • The Facebook, TikTok and Instagram icons have no text label, so screen readers can't announce them.
  • Menu items are marked up as headings, the copyright line is a heading, and the menu, hours and addresses repeat two or three times. Screen-reader users hear all of it.
  • Phone numbers aren't tap-to-call. Basket text reads "Total items in basket: 0 0".
Medium

Small errors that add up

  • "Utillita Arena" is misspelt in the footer of every page.
  • The brand appears as "GameGuru" and "Game Guru", and as "Cafe" and "Café". Menu labels mix "play", "cafe" and "shop" with "Home", "Library" and "Contact". The menu says "Loyalty", while the page title says "Membership".
  • The footer says © 2025. The theme-colour tag is empty. Collection images include "Pokeemon" and "Magivplayboosters" in their filenames, and the Funko tile uses the site logo as a placeholder.

Part 2 · Overhaul

The idea behind the new site

Today the site answers "what can I buy?". The new site should answer the question every visitor actually arrives with: "what can I do at Game Guru, and how do I lock it in?"

The proposed positioning line:

Eat, drink, play and compete at Birmingham and Solihull's home of games, and take the game home with you.

Every proposal below is tied to one or more of five value levers. Each change has to pull at least one of them, or it isn't in the plan.

Lever 1More bookings and covers, especially off-peak
Lever 2Higher spend per visit (food, drink, retail)
Lever 3Recurring revenue (membership, leagues, singles)
Lever 4Being found (local search, social sharing)
Lever 5Less staff time on phones, emails and no-shows

Homepage, top to bottom

  1. Hero with venue switcher. One photo of a busy table with food and a game, the positioning line, and two buttons: "Book a table" and "What's on tonight".
  2. Four doors. Eat & drink, Play, Parties & groups, Shop. Each is a large tile with a single sentence and a price cue, such as "Parties from £X per child".
  3. Tonight and this week. The next six events for the selected venue, pulled live from the calendar.
  4. How a visit works. Three steps: book or walk in, pick a game with a Guru, order from the table in the app. Show the play prices here: £4, £6 or £8 per person for one to three hours.
  5. Menu highlights. Four signature dishes and two cocktails, with prices, linking to the full menu.
  6. Parties strip. Photo, packages from £X, and an "Enquire" button.
  7. Curated shelf. Six hand-picked products ("Guru picks this month"), not an alphabetical list.
  8. Membership pitch. A break-even line: "Visit twice a month and Casual Player pays for itself."
  9. Reviews and social proof. Google rating, three short quotes, and press logos.
  10. Newsletter sign-up. Choose your venue and interests.

The proposals

Each one lists what changes, why, and the value to customers and to the business. Effort is relative: S is days, M is weeks, L is a month or more of build and setup.

P1

One booking engine for tables, parties and groups

NowEffort M

ScorecardBooking 3 → 5

Game Guru already uses ResDiary for table bookings, so keep it and extend it rather than replace it. Agreed direction: a hospitality booking tool. Make every "Book" button a real link to the ResDiary booking page, so it works even before scripts load, load the widget only when someone opens it rather than on every page, and set ResDiary up for play time, deposits for groups and parties, and enquiries for large events. If ResDiary can't take party deposits or give the app API access, SevenRooms is the alternative to check. The same tool also takes party and group bookings with deposits, and links to a Shopify site through an embedded widget or a booking page. The "Book" button opens the widget pre-set to the venue the visitor has selected.

For customersBook at 11pm from the sofa, in under a minute, with a confirmation and reminder.
For the businessCaptures demand outside opening hours. Deposits cut no-shows. Fewer phone calls. You build a guest database you own.
Measure itOnline bookings per week, no-show rate, share of covers booked ahead.

New requirement

TCG singles marketplace

Confirmed direction: Game Guru sells its own singles stock. The owners would also consider an embedded trading platform for players, as long as it doesn't make Game Guru liable for the trades. The site structure above has room for both (Shop → TCG singles, Join → Sell or trade cards). Singles work very differently from sealed product, so they need their own design, tools and plan.

Model A

Game Guru sells its own singles

You buy cards from players (cash or store credit), grade them, and sell them on your site, in store, and on Cardmarket, which is the main singles marketplace in the UK and Europe.

  • You control stock, pricing and quality.
  • The usual retailer rules apply. No new regulatory set-up.
  • The main cost is staff time to buy, grade and list cards.

Confirmed. Start here.

Model B

An embedded player-to-player trading board

Players list cards for trade or sale, and arrange the deal between themselves. Game Guru never takes the money or holds the card.

  • The lowest-liability way to do this is to embed or link to a third-party marketplace that already handles payments, buyer protection and disputes (for example CardNexus or Cardmarket), or to run a listings-only board where Game Guru isn't a party to the sale.
  • No platform is completely liability-free. A board where users post listings still needs clear terms that say Game Guru isn't a party to trades, rules and moderation for fakes and scams, and a check of duties for user-to-user services under the Online Safety Act. Under-18 users need particular care.
  • Taking payments or commission would turn Game Guru into a marketplace operator. That brings split payouts, seller checks and HMRC platform reporting, so it stays out of scope for now.

Consider later as a listings-only board inside the Guru Vault (see V4).

What the singles experience needs

  • Inventory tools built for singles. A TCG inventory and pricing tool that syncs stock between your Shopify shop, Cardmarket and both venues, so a card sold at Touchwood disappears online. Shopify alone isn't designed for tens of thousands of single-card listings.
  • Card search players expect. Search by name, set, collector number, rarity, language, foil and condition, with the card image and set symbol.
  • Decklist import. Paste a decklist and add every card you stock to the basket in one step. This is the feature that keeps competitive players coming back.
  • A clear condition guide (Near Mint to Damaged) with photos, and a promise to refund or replace if a card arrives in worse condition than described.
  • Prices linked to the market, updated daily from market data, with floor prices set by you so a price crash doesn't sell cards at a loss.
  • Collect at events. "Pick up at Thursday's Lorcana night", free. This brings players in on event nights and saves postage on low-value cards.
  • Buylist and trade-in. Publish what you're buying and at what price, and pay a bonus (for example 20–30% more) if the seller takes store credit. Store credit stays in your business.
  • Wantlists and alerts. Players save the cards they're hunting and get an email when one comes in stock.
  • High-value card protection. Tracked and signed-for delivery above a set value, and authenticity checks on key cards.
For playersFinish a deck before Friday's tournament without waiting a week for post. Turn spare cards into store credit locally.
For the businessSingles usually carry better margins than sealed product. Trade-in turns old cards into credit spent with you. Every pickup is a visit that can include food and drink. It makes Game Guru the place for TCG players in the region.
Risks to plan forLabour to grade and list. Price swings when sets rotate or get reprinted. Counterfeits. Tied-up cash in stock. Start with one or two games you already run events for (One Piece and Lorcana) before scaling.

Method and limits

  • What was reviewed. The page content of the home, play, café, events, venues, membership, library, contact, shop, board games collection, one product page (Brass: Birmingham), the News blog and the sitemap, fetched on 28 October 2026. Background facts (450+ games, the menu, the app, the Touchwood opening) come from UK Games Expo and Greater Birmingham Chambers of Commerce.
  • Benchmark sources. Draughts London, Geek Retreat, Flight Club Birmingham events brochure, Zatu Games loyalty case study (published by the vendor), Cardmarket guide 2026, CardNexus marketplace launch, collection app comparison, TCG Sync, Magic Madhouse on eBay. Competitor sites were reviewed from their page content, not tested end to end. Fees quoted are as published and should be checked before any decision.
  • How speed and mobile were measured. Lighthouse 12.6 in headless Chrome on 28 October 2026, on eight pages (home, play, café, events, shop, venues, membership, library), once with mobile settings (a mid-range phone on a throttled 4G connection) and once with desktop settings. Phone screenshots were taken at 390×844 and desktop ones at 1440×900, after the page had finished loading. These are lab tests from a cloud server, run once per page. Real visitors' phones will give different numbers, so treat them as a guide to the size of the problem rather than exact figures. A few analytics and booking-widget styling files were blocked by the test network, so the real pages are, if anything, slightly heavier. Google's real-visitor data (PageSpeed Insights) was over its quota and wasn't used.
  • What the browser check confirmed. The "Book" button works. On the homepage it scrolls to a ResDiary venue picker under "Reserve to Play". On other pages, the "Book Now" headings and their #book links stay hidden. Every page loads two or three copies of the ResDiary widget whether or not anyone books, which slows each page down. The library does load in a browser, with game cards and filters, pulled from the shop's catalogue by a script, so Google still can't see it. The £4, £6 and £8 play prices, and the kids' rates (under 6 free, 6 to 14 half price), are a single image with no description, shown once per venue on the Play page. So the prices are visible to sighted visitors but not to Google or screen readers.
  • Assumptions. The revenue split between food and drink, events and parties, and retail isn't known. The report assumes the venues earn more per customer than online retail. Confirming the split with the owners is a Now task, because it should set the final priority order. That the till and booking set-up can take a third-party booking tool. That the owners' "singles marketplace" could mean either model described above. Prices for packages are left as £X for the owners to set.
